Prague, briefly

Enough history to make the streets make sense, then a plan for one day and for three. Everything starts at the front door.

The city in 300 words

Prague was a castle first. Around 880 the Přemyslid dukes built a fort on the hill above a ford of the Vltava, and the town grew on both banks under it. The Old Town got its walls and its square in the 1200s; the Jewish Town beside it is older still.

The golden age has one name: Charles IV, King of Bohemia and Holy Roman Emperor. In 1348 he founded the university and laid out the New Town, the district you are sleeping in, around three markets. The largest, now Karlovo náměstí, is four minutes away. He built the stone bridge that carries his name, and the castle at Karlštejn for his crown jewels.

Prague also invented the political defenestration. In 1419 a Hussite crowd threw the councillors out of the New Town Hall on Karlovo náměstí, starting fifteen years of religious war; in 1618 Protestant nobles threw two imperial governors out of a Castle window, starting thirty. Rudolf II had meanwhile made Prague the imperial capital for a generation, filling it with astronomers, alchemists and painters.

The baroque city you see is the Catholic rebuild after 1620. The nineteenth century added the National Revival: the National Theatre on the river, the National Museum at the top of Wenceslas Square, and Dvořák, who lived in this house from 1877 and wrote the Slavonic Dances here.

Czechoslovakia was declared on Wenceslas Square in 1918. The Nazis occupied the city in 1939; the Communists took power in 1948; Soviet tanks ended the Prague Spring in 1968. On 17 November 1989 a student march down Národní třída, ten minutes from here, was beaten by riot police, and within six weeks the regime was gone. Havel took the presidency at the Castle; the country split peacefully in 1993 and joined the EU in 2004.

One day

7:30 Walk to Charles Bridge (25 min) while it is still yours. Cross into Malá Strana.

9:00 Up to the Castle by the Old Castle Steps or tram 22. Courtyards are free; buy the ticket for St Vitus and the Golden Lane if you want interiors, and skip the rest.

12:00 Down through the Castle gardens to Malá Strana. Lunch: Lokál U Bílé kuželky (Míšeňská 12) for Czech, or Café Savoy (Vítězná 5) for something grander.

14:00 Kampa island, back over the bridge, Old Town Square and the astronomical clock (watch it once, from the back of the crowd). Josefov's synagogues if you have the energy.

18:00 Home to shower; dinner at Čestr or U Šumavy from the card.

21:00 Náplavka in summer, Parlour any season.

Three days

Day 1 as above: Bridge, Castle, Malá Strana, Old Town.

Day 2, the local city. Breakfast at Myšák. Walk down to Vyšehrad (22 min): ramparts, the rotunda, Dvořák's grave in the Slavín cemetery. Tram along the river to Národní třída; the memorial plaque to 17 November 1989 is in the arcade at Národní 16. Afternoon in Vinohrady: Riegrovy sady beer garden at sunset. Dinner at Pho Vietnam Tuan & Lan or Etnosvět.

Day 3, across the river. Metro to Karlín for Eska's bakery and brunch, then walk or tram to Letná: the metronome where Stalin's statue stood, the best view of the bridges, the beer garden. Down through Holešovice if you like contemporary art (DOX). Evening: jazz at Jazz Dock or the Czech Philharmonic at the Rudolfinum.

Rain plan. Kino Světozor with English subtitles, the Municipal Library's book tower on Mariánské náměstí, the Museum of Decorative Arts, and a long afternoon at Café Louvre with the billiards upstairs.

Three things guests get wrong

  • The Castle is a district, not a building. Two hours is plenty; the view from the gardens is the point.
  • The astronomical clock's hourly show lasts 45 seconds. Watch it once, then go inside the Old Town Hall for the tower, which is the good part.
  • The best of the city is on the far side of the river from the tourist center: Letná, Karlín, Vinohrady, Vyšehrad. Give them a day.
